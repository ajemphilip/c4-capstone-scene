NEXT STEPS

Next Steps

The next step for Section C is not to treat the six projects as finished, but to treat them as a starting point for partner use, testing, and future student work. Each team created something that can move forward in a different way.

C2 · You(th) Are HerePRINT AND TEST ACCESSIBILITY PACKAGE AT YSB’S DOWNTOWN DROP-IN
C3 · You BelongTEST EVENT MODEL AND PLATFORM THROUGH A SMALL COMMUNITY EVENT
C4 · Youth Community Connection FundPILOT YCCF PLAN WITH A SMALL GROUP OF YOUTH IN OTTAWA
C5 · Your Third SpaceMOVE TOWARD CONTROLLED PILOT AFTER SAFETY & MENTOR PROTOCOLS ARE BUILT
C6 · Partner PathTEST WITH A SMALL GROUP OF SERVICE PROVIDERS BEFORE ANY WIDER ROLLOUT

Across the section, the first real next step is a structured handoff. Partners should receive the final files, links, design materials, project logic, and recommendations in a format that is easy to use. The second step is small-scale testing. None of the projects need to launch at full size right away — a small pilot, one event, one site test, or one partner review cycle would give stronger feedback than a large rollout with too many unknowns. The third step is impact tracking. Future teams or partners should collect basic feedback on what worked, what confused users, what was hard to maintain, and whether the project actually helped the people it was meant to support.

WHO CARRIES IT FORWARD

Continuation Potential

The section has strong continuation potential because the projects are connected to real needs raised by partners. They are also practical in different ways. Some projects can continue almost immediately because they are low-cost and easy to share — C1’s campaign materials and C2’s printable information sheet do not need major new systems to be useful. Other projects have larger long-term potential but need more structure before they can continue. C3, C5, and C6 all include digital platforms, which means they need hosting, maintenance, updates, and clear ownership. C4 sits between these two groups: its tools are ready to hand off, but the full Youth Community Connection Fund needs funding, staff time, and a partner willing to run the pilot.

OWNERSHIP & RESPONSIBILITY

Ownership & Responsibility

Clear ownership is the most important condition for continuation. Without it, even strong projects can become unused links, outdated files, or good ideas with no one responsible for them.

Responsibility should also be shared with future C4 teams. A future cohort could update materials, test a pilot, improve accessibility, build missing safety protocols, or expand the projects into other cities. Still, student teams should not be the only owners. The work becomes sustainable only when a partner organization has the time, interest, and authority to carry it forward.

KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER

Knowledge Transfer

Knowledge transfer should be treated as part of the project, not something added at the end. The section created many useful assets, but those assets need context in order to survive past the course. A partner should not just receive a link — they should receive the reason behind the project, the problem it responds to, the decisions made, the feedback received, the risks identified, and the recommended next steps.

For this section, strong knowledge transfer includes final reports, editable design files, website links, source files, contact lists, user guides, partner feedback summaries, and short notes explaining what still needs work. Each team has also clearly labelled what is ready to use now and what still needs testing — C2’s information sheet is ready for a site test; C5’s mentorship model is not ready to launch until safety protocols are created.

The e-Portfolio itself is the main knowledge transfer tool. It brings the six projects together in one place and gives partners a section-level view of what was built — and helps future students avoid starting from zero. Instead of repeating the same research, future teams can build from existing work, test what has already been designed, and focus on the gaps that remain. BARRIERS & RISKS

Barriers & Risks

Capacity is the biggest barrier across the section — not lack of ideas. Many partner organizations are already stretched. Even a simple project needs someone to update files, answer questions, manage users, review feedback, or keep a website alive. If no one is assigned to this work, the project can fade quickly.

Funding is another major risk. C4’s fund, C5’s platform and café vision, C6’s coordination hub, and C3’s community events all need some level of money, staff time, or technical support. Without a funding plan, these projects may remain prototypes rather than active tools.

User testing has not yet happened. Most teams could not test directly with the people most affected by the problem because of time, ethics, and access limits. Future work should be careful not to assume that partner approval is the same as user validation. Before scaling, projects should be tested with the people they are meant to support, where this can be done safely and ethically.

Maintenance and trust are ongoing risks. Information sheets, directories, funding lists, event platforms, and service databases can become harmful if they are outdated. Digital tools raise questions about privacy, moderation, accessibility, and safety. Projects involving youth, homelessness, disability, mentorship, or AI need especially careful safeguards.

The main lesson for the future is that continuation requires more than enthusiasm. It needs ownership, time, funding, testing, and a clear handoff. Section C’s work has strong value because it gives partners usable ideas and practical starting points. Its future impact will depend on whether those starting points are picked up, tested, maintained, and adapted with the people they are meant to serve.
