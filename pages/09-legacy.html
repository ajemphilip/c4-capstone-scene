Legacy & Future

Where this work goes after us — who carries it, what remains, and what the next cohort inherits.

KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER

What We Handed Over

Each Launchpad team produced a structured knowledge transfer package, handed to its partner organization and published openly. These packages include:

Programme model documentationHOW TO RUN THE PROGRAMME
Facilitation handbooksFOR PEER LEADERS & COMMUNITY WORKERS
User research findingsWHAT WE LEARNED ABOUT THE PEOPLE WE SERVE
Technical documentationFOR DIGITAL TOOLS & PLATFORMS
Prototype files & design assetsUNDER OPEN LICENCE
Evaluation frameworksHOW TO MEASURE IMPACT ONGOING

All materials are published under a Creative Commons Attribution licence. They are designed to be adapted, not copied — by future C4 cohorts, by other universities running similar programmes, and by community organizations working on homelessness in other contexts.

CONTINUATION

Who Carries It Forward

Partner organizations retain working prototypes and have committed to ongoing testing and iteration. HICC is piloting C3’s Resource Access Portal as a complement to its existing service navigation system. 360° Kids is incorporating C6’s peer support circle model into its existing programming. Youth Services Bureau is exploring C4’s Transition Mentorship Network as a formal programme offering.

Future C4 cohorts inherit both the project prototypes and the management infrastructure — the partner relationships, the research base, the cross-team frameworks, and this portfolio. The 2025–26 cohort is positioned to build second-generation versions of all six projects rather than starting from scratch.

Stewardship leads — one management student per project, nominated by each team — have agreed to maintain contact with their partner organization for a minimum of six months post-programme, providing continuity during the handover period.

OWNERSHIP

Responsibility After Graduation

A recurring question in the final weeks of the programme was: what are our responsibilities after we leave? There is no simple answer. The C4 cohort committed to three minimum obligations:

First, responding to requests for clarification or support from partner organizations for a reasonable period after the programme ends. Second, crediting the community members, people with lived experience, and partner staff who co-created the work in any future academic or professional publication. Third, not using the projects, data, or partner relationships for commercial purposes without explicit partner consent.

These are not contractual obligations. They are ethical ones. The programme cannot enforce them; it can only instil the values that make students want to honour them.

BARRIERS & RISKS

What Could Still Go Wrong

Funding continuity is the most immediate risk. Several of the pilot programmes depend on volunteer time and partner organizational bandwidth that cannot be guaranteed beyond the academic year. Solutions that depend on sustained volunteer labour without compensation are fragile. The C5 and C2 teams have the clearest paths to institutional or grant funding; C1 and C6 face the most precarious sustainability questions.

Digital tools without maintenance plans become liabilities. C2 and C3 built technology-dependent solutions. Every digital tool requires ongoing maintenance, security updates, and user support. Without a named technical steward — either inside the partner organization or through a sustained institutional arrangement — these tools will degrade and erode trust faster than they built it.

Facilitator care in peer support programmes. C6’s most important lesson — that you cannot pour from an empty cup — applies equally to the programme as a whole. Any continuation of the peer support model requires built-in facilitator supervision, recognition, and wellbeing support. Without it, the model will burn out the very people it depends on.
