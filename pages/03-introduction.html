WHAT IS C4

The Program

C4 (Cross-Campus Capstone Classroom) is York University’s flagship interdisciplinary program, connecting final-year students with real-world challenges brought by community partners, NGOs, and industry leaders. Students from across faculties collaborate in diverse teams to research, design, and present solutions — building transferable skills while making a genuine difference in the world.

“C4 is a space where people can be people first, rather than the disciplines that we sometimes see them as.” — FRANZ NEWLAND, C4 CO-FOUNDER

Over three weeks in Summer of 2026, more than a hundred students from three universities: uOttawa, Wilfred Laurier University, and YorkU, worked together in sixteen interdisciplinary teams across three sections, seven community organizations, and hundreds of people with lived experience of homelessness. This portfolio is their collective record.

COLLABORATION

Launchpad Teams & Management Team

The management team operates as the “integration layer” of C4. Each management student is paired with a Launchpad team and serves multiple roles simultaneously: project manager, strategic advisor, and documentation lead. Daily cross-team management meetings create the synthesis layer that no single Launchpad team can see from within its own project.

The programme runs in four phases: Scoping — immersive partner introductions and field research; Design Sprint — rapid prototyping alongside community members; Pilot — live testing with real end-users; Portfolio — synthesis, reflection, and documentation for handover.

This summer, across three sections A, B, and C (COIL), sixteen Launchpad teams brought together undergraduate students from York University, the University of Ottawa, and Wilfrid Laurier University. Each team approached the homelessness challenge from a different angle, drawing on backgrounds that spanned engineering, social sciences, design, business, and beyond.

Section C (COIL) alone comprised six teams (C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, and C6), supported by ten management students. What made this structure powerful was not just its scale but its diversity: students who had never shared a classroom, let alone a discipline, were asked to build something together that none of them could have built alone.

Community partnerships were embedded into the process from day one. uOttawa students engaged directly with YSB, HICC, and Trek for Teens. YorkU students worked alongside BetterStreet and 360° Kids. Wilfrid Laurier students connected with Reena an organization working at the intersection of developmental disabilities and housing instability.

Collaboration did not stop at the section level. Management students across all three sections met regularly, sharing strategies, exchanging ideas, and learning from projects beyond their own. What emerged was not three parallel experiences but one connected learning community working toward a common purpose.
