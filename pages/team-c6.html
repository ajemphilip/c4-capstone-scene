The Project

Goals & Outcomes

What We Set Out To Achieve

Team C6 set out to address fragmentation within Ontario’s homelessness support system. Rather than treating homelessness only as a shortage of housing or services, the team identified that many supports already exist but often operate separately across agencies, sectors, funding structures, and communication channels. The goal was to improve coordination between service providers so that organizations could more easily discover one another, share information, identify service gaps, build partnerships, and strengthen the supports already available to people experiencing or at risk of homelessness. The hoped-for outcome was a provider-focused platform that could help reduce duplication, improve collaboration, and support a more connected homelessness-response ecosystem.

Strategies & Processes

How We Made It Happen

Team C6 used a systems-thinking and design-thinking process to move from a broad homelessness challenge toward a focused coordination solution. The team began with literature review, stakeholder engagement, environmental scanning, a 5 Whys analysis, and a transition gap matrix to understand where fragmentation was happening. Early solution ideas included a direct service navigation tool for individuals experiencing homelessness, but partner feedback revealed that this would overlap with existing tools such as 211 Ontario. In response, the team pivoted toward PartnerPath, a collaboration hub designed specifically for service providers. The team developed the concept through sprints, partner feedback, mockups, a coded prototype, and Demo Day testing, while also considering privacy, accessibility, ethics, funding, and long-term governance.

Key Achievements

What We Accomplished

Team C6 produced PartnerPath, a prototype digital platform designed to help homelessness-sector organizations connect and coordinate. The platform includes a provider directory, organization profiles, registration and onboarding features, a discussion forum, partnership-building tools, funding-related resources, service-gap reporting concepts, and a community insights dashboard. The team also created user journey materials, a user persona, page mockups, a semi-functioning website prototype, and a GitHub repository for future development. One of the project’s strongest achievements was the team’s decision not to collect client-level data, keeping the platform focused on organizational collaboration while reducing privacy and safety risks for vulnerable populations.

Critical Evaluation

Looking Back Critically

The project addressed the identified problem by targeting the relationships between organizations rather than attempting to create another standalone service. This was a strong response to the team’s research, which showed that fragmentation, service silos, and communication gaps limit the effectiveness of the homelessness support system. PartnerPath is valuable because it complements existing systems like 211 Ontario, HIFIS, Homeless Hub, and funding trackers instead of duplicating them. At the same time, the project remains a prototype with several unresolved questions. Long-term success would depend on who owns the platform, who verifies organizations, how information stays current, how moderation is managed, how funding is secured, and whether service providers have enough capacity to participate consistently.

Recommendations

Our Takeaways and Recommendations

Team C6 concluded that homelessness support in Ontario does not only need more services; it also needs better coordination between the services that already exist. Their main recommendation is to continue developing PartnerPath as a provider-facing collaboration tool, beginning with a small pilot involving YSB and other Ottawa-based housing and social service providers. The team recommends keeping the platform focused on organization-level information, avoiding client data, and testing whether service providers find the directory, forum, partnership matching, and funding features useful in practice. Future iterations should prioritize governance, verification, accessibility, mobile responsiveness, sustainable funding, and clear ownership before expanding across Ontario.

The Journey

Starting Point

Where It All Began

Team C6 began with the broad challenge of homelessness support in Ontario. Early research and partner conversations showed that homelessness is connected to housing, health care, income support, mental health, employment, transportation, and social services. The team initially explored how individuals experiencing homelessness could better access available supports, but as the project developed, the team realized that the deeper issue was not simply whether services existed. The more important problem was that organizations providing related supports were often disconnected from one another.

Major Developments

How Our Thinking Evolved

The team’s biggest turning point came when partner feedback challenged the usefulness of their first solution idea. A direct service navigation platform for individuals risked duplicating existing tools like 211 Ontario and raised concerns around privacy, data collection, and realistic use by people experiencing homelessness. This feedback pushed the team to return to the system-level problem they had identified: provider fragmentation. From there, the project shifted toward PartnerPath, a collaboration hub for organizations rather than clients. The final direction became clearer as the team removed client-facing features, refined the platform around service providers, and focused on collaboration, partnership development, shared learning, and service-gap identification.

Challenges

The Walls We Had to Climb

Team C6 faced several major challenges during the project. The first was scope: homelessness is a complex systems issue, and the team had to narrow their work into something realistic within a short timeline. The second was direction: the early solution overlapped with existing services, forcing the team to pivot partway through development. The third was technical feasibility, since a fully functional secure platform would require backend infrastructure, user verification, moderation, and ongoing maintenance that could not be completed during the course. The team also experienced communication and project-management challenges, especially around shared understanding, task updates, and aligning the coded prototype with the final design direction.

Lessons Learned

What We’d Do Differently

Looking back, the team would define the final problem and target user earlier. More time spent comparing existing tools at the beginning would have helped the team avoid developing a solution too similar to 211 Ontario. The team also learned the importance of clearer internal communication, stronger task accountability, and making sure all members understand the current project direction before building deliverables. Future versions of the project would benefit from a stronger technical planning process, clearer design specifications before coding, and more structured partner testing. Most importantly, the team learned that a project does not need to solve homelessness directly to be meaningful; strengthening coordination between organizations can still support better outcomes across the system.

The Legacy

Future Direction & Continuation Feasibility

Looking to the Future

PartnerPath has a realistic path forward if it begins as a small pilot rather than a full provincial rollout. The first step would be to test the platform with YSB and a small group of Ottawa-based housing, health, and social service providers. This pilot could validate whether the intake form, organization profiles, search labels, forum features, and partnership tools are actually useful to frontline and organizational users. If the pilot succeeds, the platform could expand regionally across Ontario, with stronger moderation, organization verification, a real database, and improved mobile or tablet access. Continuation is feasible, but only if a committed organization or funder takes responsibility for hosting, maintaining, and governing the platform.

Responsible Interest Holders

Who We’d Work With

The most important interest holders are homelessness-sector service providers and frontline workers, since they are the intended users of PartnerPath. YSB is a natural early partner because the team already identified it as a possible pilot collaborator. HICC, BetterStreet, Trek for Teens, municipal governments, community-based organizations, housing providers, health care providers, and social service agencies could also play important roles in testing, funding, or expanding the platform. Platform administrators, web developers, and data-governance specialists would be needed to build and maintain a secure version. Funders and policymakers could also benefit from aggregated service-gap insights, as long as the platform remains careful not to expose sensitive client-level information.

Barriers

Future Mountains to Climb

The largest barrier is ownership. Without a clear organization responsible for maintaining the platform, verifying users, moderating discussions, and keeping information updated, PartnerPath could quickly become outdated or underused. Funding is another major challenge, since a secure and scalable platform would require development costs, hosting, technical support, and staff capacity. Service-provider participation is also uncertain: organizations may support collaboration in principle but struggle to update profiles or engage in forums because of limited time and competing demands. Other barriers include privacy concerns, fake accounts, governance rules, moderation needs, accessibility requirements, and the challenge of proving that improved provider collaboration leads to better outcomes for people experiencing homelessness.
