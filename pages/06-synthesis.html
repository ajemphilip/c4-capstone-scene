Cross-Project Synthesis

What the six projects say when read together — the patterns, tensions, and insights that only become visible from across the whole.

COMMON THREADS

Common Threads

Every team, regardless of starting theme, arrived at the same foundational conclusion: homelessness is not a single problem with a single solution. It is a system of interconnected failures — financial, institutional, relational, informational — and any effective intervention must choose one entry point carefully rather than attempt to solve the whole. All six teams made this pivot explicitly.

C1 moved from “how do we fix this?” to “what does it cost us not to?”REFRAME: COST OF INACTION
C2 moved from building new tools to redesigning ones that already existedREFRAME: REDESIGN OVER REBUILD
C3 moved from a single platform to a paired event-and-platform modelREFRAME: HYBRID INTEGRATION
C4 moved from standardized exit strategies to a flexible community fundREFRAME: FLEXIBILITY OVER STANDARDIZATION
C5 moved from two separate deliverables to one integrated ecosystemREFRAME: CONVERGENCE
C6 moved from a client-facing navigation tool to a provider-facing hubREFRAME: SYSTEM-LEVEL TARGET

A second thread running through every project is the centrality of community partner feedback. In each case, the decisive design pivots came directly from partner conversations rather than internal team deliberation. The community partners functioned less as validators and more as co-designers — consistently regardless of whether teams entered their first partner session with a formed idea or a blank slate.

Third: every team identified sustainability as a design criterion, not a post-project concern — reusable visual assets (C1), a printer-maintained information sheet (C2), a repeatable event model (C3), a partner-handoff implementation plan (C4), a scalable digital platform (C5), a pilot-first rollout approach (C6). Fourth: every team’s solution was shaped by an honest reckoning with what a two-week student project cannot do. None attempted to house anyone, fund a service, or reform a policy. This was not a failure of ambition; it was the condition for feasibility.

COMPARISONS & CONTRASTS

Comparisons & Contrasts

The six projects sit along a spectrum defined by two axes: who they serve and what layer of the system they intervene in.

On the audience axis: C1 targets the general public and policymakers; C2 targets youth with disabilities navigating shelter spaces; C3 targets both the community public and the organizations that want to engage them; C4 targets youth experiencing housing instability who are ready for community participation; C5 targets youth aged 16–26 actively transitioning out of housing insecurity; C6 targets service providers and organizations themselves. Together, the six projects form a near-complete stack — awareness, access, community integration, participation, individual preparation, and system coordination — that no single project could have achieved alone.

On the intervention layer axis: C1 operates at the narrative and advocacy level; C2 at the physical-space accessibility level; C3 at the community relationship level; C4 at the financial access and community participation level; C5 at the individual skill-building and mentorship level; C6 at the organizational infrastructure level. Each layer is necessary for the others to function: C6’s coordination enables C3’s event discovery; C4’s activity fund gives C5’s transitioning youth places to build community; C5’s mentors need C2’s accessible spaces; C1’s public awareness creates the political conditions for C4 and C5’s funding arguments.

On delivery type: C1 and C2 produced physical and printable artefacts designed for zero-infrastructure deployment. C3, C5, and C6 built digital platforms requiring ongoing hosting and organizational ownership. C4 occupies a middle ground: a detailed implementation plan and suite of support tools that are low-infrastructure to maintain but require institutional commitment to execute. A further contrast lies in the relationship between deliverable and deployment — C1 and C2 produced things that can be used today without any additional steps; C3, C4, C5, and C6 produced things that require a committed organizational host or funded pilot before real users encounter them.

WHERE DISCIPLINES CROSSED

Interdisciplinary Connections

Each team’s disciplinary composition shaped its deliverables in traceable ways. C1’s combination of Economics, Business Commerce, Communications, Design, and Computer Science produced a deliverable that is simultaneously data-credible, visually accessible, and strategically framed for policy audiences. C2’s mix produced an accessibility package that required both technical AODA compliance knowledge and human-centred design thinking — neither alone would have been sufficient. C4’s combination of Civil Engineering, Computer Science, Health (Psychology), Environmental and Urban Change, and Earth and Space Science produced a model grounded in both social science evidence and practical implementation logic. C5’s four-discipline combination produced the most literal interdisciplinary synthesis: qualitative research on financial instability became a playable simulation, and engineering spatial thinking became a physical 3D café model embedded in a digital pitch.

Across all six teams, the same structural pattern emerged: the research-oriented disciplines defined the problem and held the human complexity; the technical disciplines built the infrastructure and validated feasibility; and the design disciplines bridged the two. Teams that most actively forced translation across these three clusters — C4’s psychology-informed program model, C5’s simulation-as-research-synthesis, C2’s AODA-compliant design — produced the most integrated outputs. Teams where disciplines worked in parallel longer experienced scope drift and communication friction before finding their integrative moment.

The teams that narrowed earliest (C2, C6) had more time for depth and iteration; the teams that narrowed latest (C1, C3) identified this as a key lesson. No team secured committed ongoing funding, and all acknowledged that the sector’s structural reliance on short-term, project-based grants puts any continuation at risk. C5’s Five Whys analysis made the meta-problem most clear: the same fragmented, short-term funding model identified as a root cause of youth unpreparedness is also the funding environment the project must navigate to survive.

INTEGRATIVE INSIGHTS

Integrative Insights

Taken together, the six projects reveal something important about the homelessness system that no single project states directly: the system’s failure is not a shortage of good ideas or even a shortage of services. It is a shortage of connection — between organizations (C6), between the public and the problem (C1), between services and the people who need them (C2, C3), between youth and community participation (C4), and between youth and the life skills they need before independence becomes crisis (C5).

The most significant integrative insight is that these six severed connections are interdependent: you cannot sustain C5’s mentorship program without C6’s organizational coordination; you cannot fill C4’s activity slots without C3’s event infrastructure; you cannot make C2’s accessible information sheet useful if C5’s youth never know the service exists; you cannot secure funding for any of these without C1’s public awareness shifting how the problem is understood by the taxpayers and policymakers who control the budget. A second integrative insight: every team found its most important design decision not in the brainstorm but in the pivot — the moment when partner feedback or structural reality forced a change in direction. The most valuable phase in any future iteration of this program is not ideation but the transition between ideation and validation. A third insight concerns the complementarity of physical and digital deliverables: C4’s hybrid approach — a digital website hosting printable resources alongside a detailed implementation plan — suggests a model worth replicating: use digital infrastructure to organize and distribute assets that are themselves low-tech enough for any partner to deploy without ongoing technical support.
