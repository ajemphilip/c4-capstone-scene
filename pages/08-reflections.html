Journey Reflections

The road from first meeting to final build — told honestly, including the wrong turns.

INITIAL CONDITIONS

Where We Started

September 2024: 30 management students, 7 external organizations, 6 interdisciplinary teams, 8 months, and zero established protocols. The cohort arrived with enthusiasm, management frameworks from prior coursework, and a significant blind spot — most had no personal experience of homelessness and no framework for working alongside people who did.

The first two weeks of the programme were deliberately uncomfortable. Partner organizations were invited to speak not as “stakeholders” but as the subject-matter experts they are. The first shift required of every student was from “I will solve this” to “I will listen to what solving this actually requires.” For some, that shift never fully arrived. For most, it arrived around Week 4.

TURNING POINTS

The Moments That Changed Direction

Week 3 — Partner immersion sessions. Each team spent a full day embedded with its partner organization — visiting shelter sites, joining intake meetings, sitting in on peer support sessions. Every team came back with a different project than the one they thought they were building. The redesign was unanimous and immediate.

Week 6 — First user testing. C2 showed its prototype housing navigator to three people with recent lived experience of navigating housing applications. The session lasted 40 minutes. Two of the three couldn’t complete the first task without help. The team went back to basics. The redesigned version was three steps, not twelve. This pattern repeated, in different forms, in at least three other teams.

Week 12 — The open-source decision. A cross-team management session produced the cohort’s most important collective decision: all toolkits, frameworks, and programme models would be published under open licence. This changed the quality bar. Documentation written for an imagined future user — a worker at a shelter in Edmonton, a peer support facilitator in Halifax — is far more honest than documentation written for a grade.

BREAKTHROUGHS

What Worked Beyond Expectation

Lived experience advisors outperformed subject-matter experts. Every team that engaged people with direct lived experience of homelessness as advisors — not as research subjects but as consultants — reported richer insights, faster pivots, and stronger outcomes than teams that relied primarily on academic literature or professional expertise. The C4 programme will restructure its research phase around this finding for future cohorts.

Peer support beats professional support in C6. The PartnerPath team discovered that circles facilitated by people with lived experience of homelessness were consistently more effective than those facilitated by trained professionals without that experience. The evidence for this existed in the literature; experiencing it directly was a different kind of knowing.

Simple MVPs reached further. The teams that stripped their solutions to minimum viable form — and resisted the pressure to add features — reached more users, gathered better feedback, and maintained higher partner engagement. Complexity accumulated slowly; simplicity had to be defended actively.

SETBACKS

What Didn’t Go as Planned

One partner organisation withdrew mid-programme. In Week 9, a partner organization reduced its commitment due to internal capacity constraints. The affected Launchpad team had to pivot its partnership model on short notice, redistributing its liaison work across two remaining partners. The team handled it well; the management section learned to build contingency into partnership structures from the beginning, not as an afterthought.

Digital literacy gaps were underestimated. The C2 and C3 teams both designed initial solutions for a user with consistent smartphone access and moderate digital literacy. Field testing revealed this assumption was incorrect for a significant portion of the intended audience. Retrofitting accessibility into an already-designed product is harder and less effective than designing for the least-digitally-equipped user first.

The management section’s synthesis role was unclear until Week 8. For the first half of the programme, the management section operated primarily as project managers for individual teams, rather than as a synthesis function across the cohort. The value of the cross-team integration layer was only fully realized when the management students began treating their shared meetings as strategic sessions rather than status updates.
