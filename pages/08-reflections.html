Journey Reflections

The road from first meeting to final build — told honestly, including the wrong turns.

INITIAL CONDITIONS

Initial Conditions

Across all six teams, the C4 journey began with a broad, complex shared starting point, as each group approached homelessness through a different lens. Multidisciplinary student teams first needed to build familiarity and trust with one another, then align their diverse perspectives into a cohesive direction for action. Unlike traditional academic assignments with clearly defined boundaries and deliverables, teams were tasked with simultaneously shaping their problem definition, designing their approach, and executing their projects in real time.

Initial exploration reflected this openness, with teams proposing ambitious solutions and forming early ideas about what impactful deliverables might look like. C1 considered an integrated affordability platform and an employment placement program. C2 explored ramps, GIS tools, and physical accessibility infrastructure. C3 wanted to shift public stigma around homelessness at a city scale. C4 drafted a standardized cross-system exit protocol for youth transitioning out of homelessness services. C5 proposed two standalone products simultaneously — a mentorship program and a printed survival guidebook. C6 designed a client-facing navigation platform for individuals experiencing homelessness.

However, these early stages quickly revealed shared constraints: the compressed timeline made it clear that large-scale solutions would be difficult to realize, while gaps in knowledge about real-world contexts highlighted the need to engage thoughtfully with community partners. Every team arrived at the same reckoning — the first task was not to answer the question they had been given, but to find the right question to ask.

TURNING POINTS

Turning Points

Throughout the course, team momentum shifted from independent ideation toward collaborative learning, creation, and evaluation, where teams relied on partner insight to ground their work in actual needs and feasible directions. The most critical shifts in each team’s journey were driven by community partner feedback and real-world insight. These pivots fundamentally reshaped project direction and final deliverables.

C1 stopped asking “how do we solve homelessness?” and started asking “what happens if we don’t?” — a reframe that emerged directly from partner conversations with BetterStreet, YSB, and HICC, and produced a data storytelling campaign grounded in economic evidence rather than moral appeal.

C2’s defining pivot came when a YSB representative flagged in the first partner presentation that their existing information sheets for the Drop-in centre were not working. That single piece of feedback reoriented the whole team — from building something new to redesigning something that already existed and already had a partner willing to use it.

C3 received a warning from BetterStreet that an open feedback channel could become chaotic and unmoderated. The team pivoted to structured anonymized surveys the same day — a pattern of overnight iteration that characterized the team’s entire sprint cadence.

C4’s turn came from an in-person visit to YSB Ottawa: seeing the shelter and drop-in environment directly, and hearing from YSB staff that discretionary funding for community-building activities was a concrete, unmet need, shifted the project away from standardization entirely. An abstract system-level idea became a specific, fundable fund proposal.

C5 consolidated two separate deliverables into one integrated ecosystem after partner feedback from six organizations revealed that youth needed more than information alone — they needed practice, relationships, and access, all in one place.

C6’s biggest turning point came when partner feedback challenged the usefulness of their first solution. A client-facing navigation platform risked duplicating 211 Ontario and raised concerns around privacy and realistic use by people experiencing homelessness. The team returned to the system-level problem and built PartnerPath for providers instead.

Across all teams, progress was not linear and required students to let go of initial assumptions, embrace community partner input, and iterate on their project deliverables — often even overnight.

WHAT WORKED BEYOND EXPECTATIONS

Challenges and Breakthroughs

The C4 journey included a variety of shared challenges that ultimately enabled key breakthroughs. Scope management and the inability to test with real users were the most persistent difficulties, as teams struggled to narrow their focus and could not verify their solutions’ effectiveness with primary users — relying instead on partner feedback, which was not always consistently provided by the same community partners, and secondary research.

The nature of hybrid work, demanding student schedules, and interdisciplinary collaboration also introduced communication and task delivery challenges, requiring teams to prioritize progress and sometimes compromise on ideas across research, design, and technical perspectives. Teams faced additional hurdles such as technical failures, coordination latency between institutions, the need to manage multiple deliverables on short timelines, and navigating budgetary constraints.

SUCCESSES AND SETBACKS

Successes and Setbacks

Despite these challenges, all teams achieved meaningful, partner-validated outcomes, demonstrating strong creativity and interdisciplinary integration. Deliverables ranged from immediately usable tools to more integrated system-level proposals. C2’s accessibility package can be printed and deployed at YSB’s Downtown Drop-In today, without any additional infrastructure. C1’s campaign assets are already in the hands of partner organizations for redistribution. C4’s Community Compass provides a live directory of free Ottawa activities that youth can access independent of the broader YCCF pilot. C5 delivered a functioning website and a playable simulation game rebuilt under deadline pressure. C3 deployed a live platform on Vercel. C6 built a semi-functional PartnerPath prototype with a GitHub repository for future development.

These successes reflect a collective ability to translate research and community partner insights into tangible outputs within a limited timeframe. At the same time, it is recognized that each project experienced setbacks related to team dynamics and project deliverables that often became ongoing challenges to overcome. The gap between a compelling prototype and a deployable service was the most consistent setback across C3, C4, C5, and C6 — all four produced outputs that require a committed organizational host, a funded pilot, or a defined governance model before real users encounter them at scale. The mentor retention problem in C5, the ownership question in C6, the funding dependency in C4, and the event lead-time problem in C3 were all identified but not resolved within the course.

A compelling prototype is not the same as a deployable product. The gap between what was built and what can safely run is the work that remains — and naming that gap honestly is itself a form of success. CROSS-TEAM REFLECTION · C4 SECTION C
KEY LESSONS LEARNED

Key Lessons Learned

Several core lessons emerged consistently across all teams based on our journey reflections:

Early and intentional team cohesion and scope narrowing are essential to move from broad ideas to actionable solutionsLESSON 1 · NAMED BY ALL 6 TEAMS
Partner engagement must be treated as a central design driver, not a final validation step — the most impactful decisions came directly from partner inputLESSON 2 · NAMED BY C2, C4, C5, C6
Feasibility and legacy planning must be integrated into the design process, with clear consideration of ownership, maintenance, and long-term continuation from the outsetLESSON 3 · NAMED BY C2, C4, C5, C6
Interdisciplinary collaboration requires active management, ongoing communication, and empathy — it does not happen automatically and will not happen if it is not intentionally fosteredLESSON 4 · NAMED BY C3, C5, C6
Impact lies in targeted contributions rather than attempting to solve the entire system — each project’s value came from strengthening a specific pain point within the broader homelessness contextLESSON 5 · NAMED BY ALL 6 TEAMS

These five lessons are not independent. Lesson 1 (scope) enables Lesson 2 (partner centrality): you cannot act on partner feedback until you know what problem you are trying to solve. Lesson 2 enables Lesson 3 (feasibility): partners surface operational realities that make legacy planning concrete rather than theoretical. Lesson 3 depends on Lesson 4 (interdisciplinary management): ownership and handoff questions are cross-disciplinary by nature and require the whole team to negotiate them. And all four together enable Lesson 5 (targeted impact): a well-scoped, partner-grounded, operationally planned, interdisciplinary team working on a specific pain point is the formula for work that outlasts the course.
