Integrative Reflection

Six projects. Seven partners. One shared problem. What this cohort built, what it learned, and why it matters beyond the classroom.

I · MAJOR TAKEAWAYS

Major Takeaways

Six teams set out to address homelessness and housing insecurity in Ontario. None of them solved it. What they did instead was something harder to measure and more durable in practice: they each found one specific place where the system fails a specific person, and they built something that makes that failure less likely. That is the correct unit of ambition for a student project working on a problem of this scale.

C1 identified that public indifference to homelessness is partly a failure of framing — and built an evidence-based campaign that reframes inaction as a cost, not a moral choice. C2 identified that arriving at a shelter is already overwhelming, and that inaccessible information compounds that overwhelm — and built a tool that removes one barrier at exactly the right moment. C3 identified that stigma isolates people from the community that could support them — and built infrastructure for connection. C4 identified that youth have the desire to participate in community life but not the means — and proposed a fund that removes the financial barrier to belonging. C5 identified that the gap between leaving a shelter and sustaining independence is where most long-term instability begins — and built a platform to practice crossing it. C6 identified that service providers work in silos and that coordination failure costs people who can least afford it — and built a hub to begin closing that gap.

Together, these six interventions cover the homelessness response system from end to end: awareness, access, community, participation, preparation, and coordination. No single team saw that map while building. The map only became visible when the work was read as a whole. That visibility is itself a takeaway: the value of a cohort is not six independent outputs. It is one system, assembled from six entry points.

II · LESSONS FOR MANAGERS

Lessons for Managers

This capstone was a management education in the most literal sense: it required students to manage across disciplines, across institutions, across power differentials, and across the gap between what they knew and what the problem actually required. The lessons it produced are not about homelessness specifically. They are about what management is for.

Real problems do not wait for full information. Every team had to make design decisions before they fully understood the context. The teams that moved — made a choice, got feedback, revised — produced better outcomes than those that waited for certainty. Management in complex social systems is not the application of known solutions. It is the disciplined navigation of uncertainty toward action.

Relationships precede results. The single most consistent predictor of project quality across all six teams was the depth of the relationship with the community partner. Not the sophistication of the framework. Not the technical capability of the build. The relationship. This challenges a foundational assumption of much management education, which treats relationships as soft infrastructure and technical skill as the core competency. In this cohort, it was the reverse.

Management is translation work. C5’s breakthrough moment — turning qualitative research on financial instability into coded game mechanics — required a manager willing to sit between the researcher and the developer and hold both until they found each other’s language. C4’s Kumar and Kay did this across four sprints, preventing scope creep not by controlling the work but by continuously re-anchoring it to the problem. C2’s team did it between AODA compliance standards and human-centred design. In every case, the management function was less about directing and more about translating — across disciplines, across organizations, and across the gap between what was designed and what was needed.

Feasibility is an ethical question, not just a practical one. When C6 discovered their client-facing navigation platform risked duplicating 211 Ontario and exposing vulnerable users to privacy risks, the decision to pivot was not primarily about saving time. It was about not building something that could harm the people it was meant to help. Every management decision in this capstone carried that weight. Choosing a scope, a partner, a platform, a feature — each choice had a human on the other end of it. Holding that awareness is what distinguishes management in social systems from management in commercial ones.

The management challenge of this capstone was relationship architecture — building the trust infrastructure that allows solutions to work, the coordination infrastructure that allows teams to learn from each other, and the narrative infrastructure that allows a portfolio of six projects to tell one coherent story. III · IMPACT OF PROJECTS & PARTNERSHIPS

Impact of Projects & Partnerships

Impact in this context means something specific. It does not mean scale. It means that something changed, in a real place, for a real person or organization, that would not have changed without this work.

By that measure, impact was achieved. YSB received an accessibility package designed for their specific Downtown Drop-In space that their staff can print, update, and maintain without design expertise — a tool that did not exist before C2 built it. The Ottawa region now has a mapped directory of 32+ youth activity providers across five categories, with a free-activities Community Compass and a funded participation model, because C4 made an in-person visit and asked the right question. Six community partners engaged seriously enough with these projects to shape them — not as feedback-givers at the end, but as co-designers throughout. That level of partner engagement is not guaranteed in a student programme; it was earned.

The partnerships themselves were a form of impact. HICC, YSB, BetterStreet, Trek for Teens, Reena, 360 Kids, and York University Community Safety each brought their operational knowledge into contact with student design energy. In most cases, that contact produced something neither side would have produced alone. The partners surfaced needs the teams could not have found in the literature. The teams produced documentation, prototypes, and analytical frameworks the partners did not have the time or resources to build themselves. The exchange was genuinely mutual.

It is the inheritance.

IV · BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Broader Implications

This cohort worked on homelessness. But the implications of what it learned extend well beyond any single policy domain. They concern how universities engage with social problems, how management education prepares students for complexity, and how institutions can be more useful partners to the communities that host them.

Universities can be more useful than they typically are. The dominant model of university-community engagement is extractive: students study a community, write a paper, and leave. C4 inverted this. Students stayed in contact with partners across the full course. Deliverables were designed for handoff, not submission. The e-Portfolio is a public document, not an archived grade. This model is replicable. It requires institutional commitment to partner relationships that persist beyond a single term, and a willingness to let the problem shape the curriculum rather than the curriculum shape the problem. Both are available. What is often missing is the will.

Homelessness is a systems problem that requires systems thinking — and systems actors. Each of the six projects addressed one node in a connected system. C1 shifts how the public understands the problem. C2 makes the entry point less overwhelming. C3 builds the community connections that prevent re-entry. C4 funds the participation that builds belonging. C5 prepares youth for independence before they are forced into it. C6 coordinates the organizations that deliver all of the above. No single intervention is sufficient. The implication for policy is that fragmented, project-based funding — the same funding model that C5’s Five Whys analysis identified as a root cause of youth unpreparedness — is structurally mismatched to a problem that requires coordinated, sustained response. Funding one node at a time does not build a system. It builds six orphaned projects.

The next generation of managers is ready for this work — if education prepares them for it. Every student in this cohort arrived with frameworks and left with something harder to teach: the judgment to know when a framework applies and when the situation requires something it cannot supply. The willingness to sit with ambiguity. The discipline to narrow scope under pressure. The humility to let a YSB staff member redirect six weeks of work in twenty minutes — and to be grateful for it. These are not soft skills. They are the core competencies of management in conditions that matter.

CLOSING

Not a Record of Completion

This portfolio documents six student projects. It also documents something larger: what happens when a university takes a real problem seriously, builds real partnerships, and trusts students to navigate real complexity in real time. The answer, in this case, is that something genuine gets built — imperfect, incomplete, but genuinely useful. And that something has a foundation now that it did not have before.

If you are reading this as a future C4 student, a community partner, or a funder considering whether to support this kind of work: the six projects in this portfolio are starting points, not endpoints. C2’s information sheet is ready to print. C1’s campaign is ready to share. C4’s implementation plan is ready to pilot. C3, C5, and C6 are ready for the organizational hosts that will take them from prototype to practice. What they are not ready for is to be filed away. The problem they were built to address is still here. The people it affects did not wait for the course to end.

Hobbies should not be a privilege. Securing housing is only the beginning, not the finish line. The right information, in the right place, for every person who walks through the door. The supports exist — they just don’t know each other yet. Visualizing the hidden costs of housing insecurity and homelessness. Bringing residents and organizations into the same room, and keeping them there. These are not taglines. They are six arguments for why this work matters — and six commitments to the people it is meant to serve. C4 LAUNCHPAD & MANAGEMENT TEAMS · COIL (SECTION C) · SUMMER 2026
