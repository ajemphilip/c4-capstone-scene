OVERALL ACHIEVEMENTS

Overall Achievements

Across six teams, the Section C cohort produced a diverse and complementary set of deliverables that collectively cover the homelessness response system from public narrative to individual skill-building to organizational coordination. Measured against the scope available — two-week sprints, student teams, no capital budget — these achievements represent genuine, partner-validated contributions to a sector that typically moves much more slowly.

EFFECTIVE STRATEGIES & PROCESSES

Effective Strategies & Processes

Partner feedback as design input, not validation. The most effective teams treated community partner conversations as formative rather than confirmatory. C2’s pivot toward YSB’s existing information sheet, C4’s turn toward the YCCF after the YSB Ottawa site visit, C5’s consolidation of two deliverables into one ecosystem, and C6’s move from client-facing to provider-facing design all came from partner feedback that challenged working assumptions. Teams that sought this feedback earliest and acted on it fastest produced more targeted and feasible solutions.

Parallel sub-team structures. All six teams divided into research/design and technical/development tracks working concurrently rather than sequentially. This was most explicitly described by C4 (with distinct roles across research, implementation writing, website development, and graphic design) and C5 (research vs. technical sub-teams). Parallel structures eliminated the bottleneck of waiting for research to complete before building began.

Five Whys analysis for root-cause clarity. Used by C2, C3, C4, C5, and C6, the Five Whys process consistently surfaced root causes that initial brainstorming missed. For C4 it distinguished community involvement from standardization as separable problems; for C5 it distinguished educational fragmentation from structural fragmentation; for C6 it identified provider disconnection rather than service scarcity. Teams that completed this analysis early had more focused problem statements and faster solution convergence.

Dedicated project management as a separate layer. Teams with explicit management roles — C5 with Kay and Kumar; C4 with Colin Gordon in a task planning and delegation role — reported better scope control, more consistent accountability, and smoother conflict resolution. The value of a management layer not also responsible for deliverables was underscored by C5’s reflection that their managers prevented overthinking and kept scope realistic at critical junctures.

Designing contingency deliverables. C4’s decision to develop the Community Compass and Interest Flowchart as standalone tools — explicitly framed as fallbacks should the full YCCF pilot not be implemented — is a strategy that deserves recognition and replication. A “useful regardless of full implementation” design criterion should be built into every project from the start.

In-person partner engagement. C4’s YSB Ottawa site visit produced the defining insight of their entire project — the discretionary funding gap — in a way that no remote conversation had. C2’s YSB partnership was similarly anchored in direct site observation. Physical presence in a partner organization’s environment surfaced operational realities that survey responses and presentations cannot replicate.

CONTINUE / START / STOP REFLECTIONS

Continue / Start / Stop Reflections

CONTINUE
  • Engaging community partners as co-designers from the first session — not as evaluators at the final presentation
  • Using Five Whys and structured root-cause analysis before generating solutions
  • Building parallel research and technical sub-teams to eliminate sequential bottlenecks
  • Narrowing scope deliberately and early — every team that did this had more time for depth and partner validation
  • Designing continuation and handoff into deliverables from the outset, so partners can own the work after the cohort ends
  • Maintaining interdisciplinary integration as an active, managed process — not an assumed byproduct of team composition
  • Deploying dedicated project management as a layer separate from deliverable contributors
  • Conducting in-person or site-visit partner engagement where possible
  • Designing contingency or standalone deliverables alongside the main output
START
  • Pursuing ethics approval for direct user testing from week one — a stated limitation across all six projects
  • Defining operational backends — governance, safety protocols, maintenance ownership, staff allocation, funding pathways — alongside deliverables, not as a next-phase concern
  • Mapping how projects across teams interconnect at the start of the cohort, to design for complementarity and avoid duplicating existing tools
  • Building impact measurement into each project: clear indicators, baseline data, and a feedback mechanism that survives the handoff
  • Establishing explicit retention and sustainability mechanisms for any solution relying on sustained human participation
  • Designing for the lowest-capacity user from the start, including a staff-facilitated pathway for users least able to self-advocate
  • Conducting a comparative audit of existing tools before ideation, to avoid inadvertently replicating platforms already deployed in the sector
STOP
  • Spending early sprints evaluating solution ideas that are clearly infeasible — all six teams named this as lost time
  • Treating scope as a flexible boundary that will eventually settle on its own — it requires active management decisions
  • Allowing disciplines to work in parallel without structured translation checkpoints — interdisciplinary synthesis does not happen automatically
  • Designing platforms without defining who owns them, who maintains them, and how information stays current after the student team concludes
  • Delaying communication infrastructure setup until friction has already developed — shared boards and check-in rhythms should be established in the first session
  • Building solutions where the primary bottleneck is organizational capacity to participate without first confirming users have time and institutional support to engage
  • Documenting uneven contribution after the fact — workload imbalances should be caught and corrected mid-sprint, not surfaced only in the final report
